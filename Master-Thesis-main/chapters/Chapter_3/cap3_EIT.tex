\section{Numerical Simulation Results of EIT Cooling}
\label{sec:eit_cooling_results}
%*###############################################################

The numerical model described in Section~\ref{sec:numerical_eit} was evaluated to characterize the Electromagnetically Induced Transparency (EIT) cooling dynamics of $^{87}\mathrm{Rb}$ atoms. To distinguish the fundamental cooling mechanism from the effects of the complex atomic structure, the simulations were performed in two stages. First, an idealized three-level $\Lambda$ system was simulated to validate the analytical predictions. Subsequently, a complete 24-level model incorporating the full hyperfine and Zeeman structure of the $D_2$ line was implemented to assess realistic experimental conditions, including off-resonant excitation and population leakage.

%*###############################################################
\subsection{Ideal three-level system dynamics}
\label{subsec:results_three_level}
%*###############################################################

The steady-state spectroscopic response of the ideal three-level system was obtained by scanning the probe-laser detuning $\Delta_p$ while keeping the coupling field fixed. The resulting total excited-state population exhibits the characteristic Fano profile associated with EIT. Figure~\ref{fig:fano_3_combined} shows both the macroscopic transparency window and a detailed view of the sideband alignment.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Cap_3_figures/fano_full_3.png}

\vspace{0.5cm}
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{Cap_3_figures/fano_3.png}
\caption{Steady-state excited population profile of the idealized three-level system. Top: Full spectrum showing the transparency window (dashed red line) at the two-photon resonance. Bottom: Detailed profile near the dark state, showing the narrow absorption maximum aligned with the red sideband (solid red line) and the carrier transition falling in the transparency minimum (dashed green line).}
\label{fig:fano_3_combined}
\end{figure}

The coupling laser intensity was set according to the exact cooling condition (Eq.~\eqref{eq:exact_condition_numerical}), tuning the AC Stark shift so that the absorption maximum precisely overlaps with the cooling transition. The time evolution of the average phonon number $\langle n \rangle$ for this configuration is presented in Figure~\ref{fig:time_evol_3}. Starting from an initial thermal state, the system undergoes rapid ground-state cooling, decaying exponentially and approaching the theoretical minimum limit in less than 0.1 ms. This confirms the efficiency of the targeted sideband asymmetry in the absence of competing decay channels.

To demonstrate the necessity of internal state management, the dynamics were first simulated without an active repump field, and then compared with the full optimized sequence. The impact of optical pumping is evident in the steady-state spectroscopy (Figure~\ref{fig:fano_24_comparison}). Without a repump field, the excited-state population drops to near zero across the entire frequency scan. Following a few excitation cycles, the atoms decay into uncoupled Zeeman sublevels, completely depleting the active EIT levels.

By introducing a $\pi$-polarized repump field, the atoms are recycled back into the EIT cycle. The Fano profile and the dark resonance are recovered, allowing the alignment of the red sideband with the absorption peak. However, compared to the three-level model, the resonance is broader and the transparency minimum is less deep, owing to off-resonant coupling to adjacent excited manifolds and the continuous scattering induced by the repump field.

This optical pumping mechanism is directly observable in the time evolution of the internal atomic populations (Figure~\ref{fig:populations_comparison}). Without the repumper, the active ground state populations decay rapidly, accumulating entirely in the uncoupled states within a few milliseconds. Conversely, activating the repump field sustains the population within the active states throughout the evolution, continuously recovering atoms that leak out of the ideal cooling cycle.

However, the effective cooling rate is drastically reduced compared to the ideal three-level case. The system requires nearly 18 ms to approach $\langle n \rangle \approx 0$, two orders of magnitude longer than the idealized simulation. First, atoms spend a significant fraction of their time outside the optimal $\Lambda$ system waiting to be recycled; second, the excess scattering from the repumper and the imperfect carrier suppression introduce heating events that must be continuously compensated. Despite this reduction in cooling speed, \CCref{the simulated cooling timescale is compatible with state-of-the-art EIT cooling experiments}{Affermazione di confronto senza il termine di paragone: converrebbe riportare il tempo di raffreddamento effettivo di \texttt{Chow2024} accanto ai 18~ms simulati. Cosi' com'e' il lettore deve fidarsi, e ``compatible'' e' esattamente il tipo di parola che un esaminatore chiede di quantificare.} implemented in free-space setups, where similar multilevel effects occur~\cite{Chow2024}.

While these results validate the EIT cooling scheme within the complete atomic level structure, the simulation must eventually be adapted to reflect the exact conditions of the fiber implementation. Atoms confined near the core surface of the hollow-core fiber will experience spatially varying tensor light shifts, local magnetic field gradients, and specific polarization constraints due to the fiber's geometry. Future modeling efforts will need to incorporate these physical perturbations into the Hamiltonian to fully optimize the laser parameters and ensure robust cooling in the final experimental environment.
