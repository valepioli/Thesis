\section{Experimental Procedures and Data Analysis}

\subsection{Cold-Atom Preparation: MOT and Optical Molasses}

Each experimental cycle begins with loading atoms into the magneto-optical trap (MOT), using the operating conditions previously identified as optimal for this apparatus. The MOT is run with an axial magnetic-field gradient of $B' = 9.32\,\mathrm{G/cm}$, corresponding to a current of $8\,\mathrm{A}$ in the quadrupole coils, a cooling-light detuning of $\Delta = -13\,\mathrm{MHz}$, and all compensation-coil currents set to zero. The loading stage lasts $5\,\mathrm{s}$.

At the end of the MOT phase, the quadrupole magnetic field is switched off and the cloud enters the optical molasses stage. In the absence of the trapping field, the atoms are no longer confined and the cloud starts to expand. At the same time, the atomic temperature decreases further, while the cloud falls under gravity and remains subject to a velocity-dependent damping force generated by the cooling light.

The temperature reached in optical molasses scales approximately as $I/|\Delta|$, where $I$ is the cooling-beam intensity and $\Delta$ is the laser detuning (see Sec.~\ref{sec:ODT}). This scaling is valid only within an appropriate detuning range. If $|\Delta|$ is too large, the photon scattering rate, $\Gamma_p \propto 1/\Delta^2$, becomes too small to sustain efficient cooling. On the other hand, if the detuning is too small, excitation of the neighbouring $F=2 \rightarrow F'=2$ transition is no longer negligible.

A second requirement for efficient molasses cooling is the cancellation of residual magnetic fields. For this reason, $5\,\mathrm{ms}$ after the MOT field is switched off, the shim-coil currents are adjusted in order to compensate for the Earth's magnetic field and for other stray fields present in the laboratory. The cooling-light detuning is then changed from the MOT value to the molasses value through a 20-step ramp with total duration $5\,\mathrm{ms}$. This ramp starts $50\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ after the shim currents are set to their molasses values.

\subsection{Atom Imaging}

The atomic cloud is observed inside the vacuum chamber by means of two monochrome CMOS cameras:

\begin{itemize}
    \item a Teledyne FLIR BFS-U3-04S2M-CS camera, based on the Sony IMX287 sensor, with a resolution of $720\times540$ pixels and a pixel size of $6.9\,\mu\mathrm{m}\times6.9\,\mu\mathrm{m}$;
    \item an Allied Vision Manta G-419 NIR camera, based on the CMOSIS/ams CMV4000 sensor, with a resolution of $2048\times2048$ pixels and a pixel size of $5.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}\times5.5\,\mu\mathrm{m}$.
\end{itemize}

The FLIR camera is used mainly during the optimization of the molasses stage. The Manta camera, owing to its higher sensitivity at $780\,\mathrm{nm}$ and its smaller pixel pitch, is used for ODT loading measurements, where resolving the small trapped cloud is more demanding.

Both cameras are equipped with a Thorlabs MVL50M23 adjustable objective, with focal length $50\,\mathrm{mm}$, minimum working distance $200\,\mathrm{mm}$, and minimum magnification of about $0.33$. The imaging system is focused so that the HCPCF inside the chamber, and therefore also the atomic cloud located at nearly the same distance, is sharply imaged on the sensor. In both cases the acquisition is triggered externally by the FPGA control system.

\subsection{FLIR Camera Calibration}

A calibration of the imaging system is required in order to convert distances measured on the sensor into physical lengths in the object plane. This is needed in particular for the temperature measurements, where the cloud width extracted from the images must be expressed in absolute units. Starting from the physical pixel pitch of the FLIR sensor, $6.9\,\mu\mathrm{m}$, the optical magnification $M$ and the corresponding object-plane pixel size $C$ were estimated through three independent methods.

\begin{enumerate}

\item \textbf{Direct imaging of a known microstructure}

The first method uses the hollow-core photonic crystal fiber (HCPCF) as a reference object, exploiting its known physical diameter of $316\,\mu\mathrm{m}$. The fiber is imaged directly and its apparent diameter on the sensor is compared with the real one. Since the image is affected by optical blur and the fiber edges cannot be located accurately by eye, the diameter is extracted from a spatial fit.

The fitted image corresponds to an apparent diameter of 8 pixels. The resulting object-plane calibration is therefore
\begin{equation}
    C = \frac{316\,\mu\mathrm{m}}{8\,\mathrm{px}} = 39.5\,\mu\mathrm{m/px},
\end{equation}
which gives a magnification
\begin{equation}
    M = \frac{6.9}{39.5} \approx 0.174.
\end{equation}

\item \textbf{Optical calibration by reflected ruler imaging}

The second method relies on imaging a macroscopic reference target placed at the same optical distance as the atomic cloud. A ruler is observed through a mirror after the camera has already been focused on the atoms. The ruler is translated until its reflected image becomes sharp, ensuring that the target lies on the same focal plane as the cloud.

Several ruler intervals are measured in order to reduce the uncertainty and average over possible local imperfections. This procedure yields an effective pixel size of about $43.3\,\mu\mathrm{m/px}$, corresponding to
\begin{equation}
    M \approx 0.160.
\end{equation}

\item \textbf{Calibration from MOT displacement in a bias magnetic field}

As a further cross-check, the imaging scale can be estimated from the displacement of the MOT induced by a controlled bias magnetic field. When a uniform field is applied along the vertical direction by means of the $y$-axis shim coils, the zero of the quadrupole field is shifted, and the cloud follows the new equilibrium position.

Experimentally, the cloud displacement varies linearly with the current applied to the $y$-axis shim coils, with measured slope
\begin{equation}
    y_{\mathrm{shift}} = 58\,\mathrm{px/A}.
\end{equation}

To convert this result into a physical displacement, one needs the bias field generated per unit shim current and the restoring gradient of the quadrupole field. Modeling the shim coils as a cubic structure of side $L = 645\,\mathrm{mm}$ with $N = 100$ windings gives
\begin{equation}
    b = 1.43\,\mathrm{G/A}.
\end{equation}
The quadrupole gradient is estimated from the coil geometry. For a current of $8.4\,\mathrm{A}$, 128 windings, effective radius $R_{\mathrm{eff}} = 62\,\mathrm{mm}$, and effective separation $a_{\mathrm{eff}} = 162\,\mathrm{mm}$, the vertical gradient is
\begin{equation}
    G_{y,\mathrm{tot}} = 0.571\,\mathrm{G/mm}.
\end{equation}

The equilibrium displacement per unit current is given by the ratio between the applied bias field and the magnetic-field gradient. Dividing this physical shift by the measured displacement in pixels gives the calibration factor:
\begin{equation}
    C = \frac{b}{G_{y,\mathrm{tot}}\,y_{\mathrm{shift}}}
    = \frac{1.43\,\mathrm{G/A}}{0.571\,\mathrm{G/mm}\times58\,\mathrm{px/A}}
    \approx 0.043\,\mathrm{mm/px}
    = 43\,\mu\mathrm{m/px}.
\end{equation}

If, instead, the empirically measured values previously obtained for this setup are used, namely $b = 1.5\,\mathrm{G/A}$ and $G = 5.6\,\mathrm{G/cm}$ at $8.4\,\mathrm{A}$, one obtains
\begin{equation}
    C = \frac{1.5}{5.6\times58}\,\mathrm{cm/px} \approx 46\,\mu\mathrm{m/px}.
\end{equation}

Both estimates are consistent with the result obtained from the ruler calibration. In the following analysis, the value provided by the optical method is adopted.

\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Temperature Measurements}

Once the imaging calibration is known, the cloud size extracted from the images can be expressed in physical units and used to determine the temperature from ballistic expansion. These measurements are used to optimize the molasses parameters by identifying the conditions that minimize the final temperature.

The method is based on the free expansion of the cloud after release. For a non-interacting gas, the position of an atom along one direction evolves as
\begin{equation}
    x(t) = x_0 + v_x t.
\end{equation}
Assuming that the initial position and velocity are statistically independent, the variance at time $t$ is
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_x^2(t) = \sigma_{x,0}^2 + \sigma_{v_x}^2 t^2,
\end{equation}
where $\sigma_{x,0}$ is the initial spatial width. In the center-of-mass frame, the equipartition theorem gives
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_{v_x}^2 = \langle v_x^2 \rangle = \frac{k_B T_x}{m}.
\end{equation}
The fitting law then becomes
\begin{equation}
    \sigma_x^2(t) = \sigma_{x,0}^2 + \frac{k_B T_x}{m} t^2,
    \label{eq:ballistic_expansion}
\end{equation}
with $m$ the mass of $^{87}\mathrm{Rb}$, $m \approx 1.443\times10^{-25}\,\mathrm{kg}$.

The experimental procedure is as follows:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item A MOT is loaded and followed by the molasses stage. The molasses parameters and duration are the quantities under optimization.
    \item The cooling light is switched off, allowing the cloud to expand freely and fall under gravity. At each selected time of flight, the FLIR camera acquires a fluorescence image of the cloud.
    \item A background frame is then acquired after a sufficiently long delay, so that the cloud has completely left the observation region.
    \item For each time of flight, the corrected image is obtained as
    \begin{equation}
        I(t) = I_{\mathrm{raw}}(t) - I_{\mathrm{bg}}(t).
    \end{equation}
    \item Each corrected image is fitted with a two-dimensional Gaussian, yielding the widths $\sigma_x$ and $\sigma_y$.
    \item Equation~\ref{eq:ballistic_expansion} is fitted independently along the two axes to extract $T_x$ and $T_y$. The temperature quoted in the following is the average of the two values.
\end{enumerate}

Since the imaging pulse destroys the sample, each time-of-flight point must be acquired in a separate experimental cycle.

\subsection{ODT Loading Measurements}

The loading performance of the optical dipole trap is characterized using the Manta camera. Two quantities are extracted from these measurements: the loading efficiency $\eta$ and the integrated fluorescence signal associated with the trapped cloud, which is proportional to the number of captured atoms.

Because only a small fraction of the molasses atoms, of the order of $10^{-3}$, is transferred into the ODT, the trapped-atom signal is much weaker than the fluorescence produced by the untrapped cloud. For this reason, the analysis is based on differential imaging. The sequence for loading the atoms and their subsequent free-fall is illustrated in Figure \ref{fig:loading_free_fall}.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\linewidth]{Cap_2_figures/loading_free_fall.png}
    \caption{Schematic representation of the experimental sequence for loading and guiding atoms. The process consists of four stages: (1) preparation of the cold atomic cloud, (2) loading the atoms into the far-off-resonance trap (FORT) using a trapping beam, (3) free-falling of the atomic cloud under gravity, and (4) guiding of the optically trapped atoms into the hollow-core fiber (HCF), while the untrapped atoms fall and disperse outside the trap. Credits~\cite{Wang2025}.}
    \label{fig:loading_free_fall}
\end{figure}

The measurement sequence is the following:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Two otherwise identical experimental cycles are performed in succession, one with the ODT on from the MOT loading stage onward and one with the ODT off.
    \item After a time of flight of $30\,\mathrm{ms}$, long enough for the untrapped atoms to expand away from the ODT region, fluorescence images $I_{\mathrm{on}}$ and $I_{\mathrm{off}}$ are acquired with the Manta camera.
    \item Each on/off pair is repeated five times.
    \item The images are cropped to a pixel region of interest centered on the cloud. This excludes both the visible HCPCF structure and the peripheral parts of the image containing only background.
    \item For each condition, the cropped images are averaged:
    \begin{equation}
        \bar I_{\mathrm{on}} = \langle I_{\mathrm{on}} \rangle,
        \qquad
        \bar I_{\mathrm{off}} = \langle I_{\mathrm{off}} \rangle.
    \end{equation}
    \item The averaged images are integrated along one direction to obtain one-dimensional profiles with improved signal-to-noise ratio.
    \item The total number of atoms in the molasses drifts slowly over time because of variations in vacuum pressure and thermal changes in the optical components. As a consequence, the on and off profiles cannot be subtracted directly: even a small mismatch in the broad molasses background would produce a residual comparable to the ODT signal itself.
    
    To correct for this effect, the off profile is rescaled by a factor $k$ chosen so that the difference between the two profiles vanishes outside the ODT region. An exclusion window centered at the ODT position, $x_{\mathrm{ODT}} \pm R_{\mathrm{window}}$, is defined. Outside this interval, a linear baseline $L_i(x)$ is estimated for each profile and the residual integrated signal is computed as
    \begin{equation}
        A_i^{\mathrm{out}} = \int_{\mathrm{outside}} \left[P_i(x) - L_i(x)\right]\,dx,
        \qquad i \in \{\mathrm{on,off}\}.
    \end{equation}
    The normalization factor is then
    \begin{equation}
        k = \frac{A_{\mathrm{on}}^{\mathrm{out}}}{A_{\mathrm{off}}^{\mathrm{out}}}.
    \end{equation}
    
    \item The differential profile attributed to the trapped atoms is therefore
    \begin{equation}
        \Delta \bar I(x) = \bar I_{\mathrm{on}}(x) - k\,\bar I_{\mathrm{off}}(x).
    \end{equation}
    
    \item A Gaussian plus linear background is fitted both to the off profile and to the differential profile. If $A_{\mathrm{off}}^{\mathrm{G}}$ and $A_{\mathrm{ODT}}^{\mathrm{G}}$ denote the Gaussian areas extracted from the two fits, the loading efficiency is defined as
    \begin{equation}
        \eta = \frac{A_{\mathrm{ODT}}^{\mathrm{G}}}{k\,A_{\mathrm{off}}^{\mathrm{G}}}.
    \end{equation}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Measurement of the Number of Atoms}

The loading efficiency can be obtained from relative fluorescence signals and therefore does not require an absolute calibration of the camera response. By contrast, estimating the absolute number of trapped atoms requires converting the recorded counts into a known number of detected photons.

To this end, the camera is calibrated against a beam of known optical power derived from the MOT light. The beam is directed onto the sensor after passing through a series of attenuators and through the vacuum-chamber viewports. The power measured along the optical path includes a constant background contribution of $P_{\mathrm{bg}} = 1.5\,\mu\mathrm{W}$, which is subtracted from all radiometric readings.

The total transmission of the optical path is written as the product of the individual transmission factors,
\begin{equation}
    T_{\mathrm{tot}} = T_1\,T_{\mathrm{vac}}\,T_2\,T_3 \approx 0.01272.
\end{equation}
During the calibration, the AOM amplitude is set to 50, corresponding to an input power of $22\,\mu\mathrm{W}$ after background subtraction. The optical power reaching the camera is therefore
\begin{equation}
    P_{\mathrm{cam}} = 22\,\mu\mathrm{W}\times T_{\mathrm{tot}} \approx 279.8\,\mathrm{nW}.
\end{equation}

\begin{wrapfigure}{r}{0.5\textwidth}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Cap_2_figures/manta_calib.png}
    \caption{Linear fit of the total camera counts as a function of the exposure time $t_{\mathrm{exp}}$. The slope $m$ extracted from the fit is used to calculate the photon conversion factor $K$.}
    \label{fig:calibration_manta}
\end{wrapfigure}

The camera response is determined by varying the exposure time $t_{\mathrm{exp}}$ while keeping $P_{\mathrm{cam}}$ fixed. As shown in Figure \ref{fig:calibration_fit}, for each image, the beam profile is fitted in two dimensions and the integrated counts are extracted. Denoting by $C$ the total number of recorded counts, the response is assumed to be linear:
\begin{equation}
    C = m\,t_{\mathrm{exp}} + q,
\end{equation}
where $m$ is the count rate. Since the incident photon flux is given by $P_{\mathrm{cam}}/(hc/\lambda)$, the conversion factor from counts to detected photons is
\begin{equation}
    K = \frac{P_{\mathrm{cam}}\lambda}{m h c},
\end{equation}
where $h$ is Planck's constant, $c$ the speed of light, and $\lambda$ the laser wavelength.

For an ODT fluorescence image, the integrated residual signal $C_{\mathrm{tot}}$ can then be converted into the number of detected photons:
\begin{equation}
    N_{\mathrm{det}} = K\,C_{\mathrm{tot}}.
\end{equation}

In order to infer the number of trapped atoms, the fluorescence contribution of a single atom during the imaging pulse must be estimated. The scattering rate is described by the standard two-level expression
\begin{equation}
    R_{\mathrm{sc}} =
    \frac{\Gamma}{2}
    \frac{I/I_{\mathrm{sat}}}{1 + I/I_{\mathrm{sat}} + 4(\Delta/\Gamma)^2},
\end{equation}
where $\Gamma$ is the natural linewidth, $I$ is the total imaging intensity, $I_{\mathrm{sat}}$ is the saturation intensity, and $\Delta$ is the laser detuning.

For an imaging pulse of duration $t_{\mathrm{img}}$, each atom scatters on average $R_{\mathrm{sc}} t_{\mathrm{img}}$ photons. Only a fraction of this fluorescence reaches the sensor. Approximating the collection efficiency by the fractional solid angle subtended by the imaging optics,
\begin{equation}
    \eta_{\mathrm{coll}} = \frac{r^2}{4d^2},
\end{equation}
with $r$ the effective lens radius and $d$ the distance between the cloud and the collection optics, the number of detected photons per atom is
\begin{equation}
    N_{\mathrm{ph,1}} = R_{\mathrm{sc}}\,t_{\mathrm{img}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{coll}}\,T_{\mathrm{optics}},
\end{equation}
where $T_{\mathrm{optics}}$ accounts for the transmission of the imaging system.

The absolute number of trapped atoms is finally obtained from
\begin{equation}
    N = \frac{N_{\mathrm{det}}}{N_{\mathrm{ph,1}}}
    = \frac{K\,C_{\mathrm{tot}}}{R_{\mathrm{sc}}\,t_{\mathrm{img}}\,\eta_{\mathrm{coll}}\,T_{\mathrm{optics}}}.
\end{equation}

\section{Expected Number of Trapped Atoms}

A simple estimate of the number of atoms loaded into the ODT can be obtained by combining the spatial density distribution of the MOT with the thermal velocity distribution of the atoms. An atom at position $\vec{r}$ is assumed to be captured if its transverse kinetic energy is smaller than the local trap depth:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{2}m\left(v_x^2 + v_y^2\right) < \left|U_{\mathrm{ODT}}(\vec{r})\right|.
\end{equation}

The spatial density of the initial cloud is denoted by $n_{\mathrm{MOT}}(\vec{r})$, while the velocity distribution is taken to be Maxwell-Boltzmann,
\begin{equation}
    f(\vec{v}) = \frac{1}{(2\pi\mu^2)^{3/2}} \exp\left(-\frac{v^2}{2\mu^2}\right),
    \qquad
    \mu^2 = \frac{k_B T}{m}.
\end{equation}

The number of captured atoms is obtained by integrating over phase space, restricting the result to atoms moving toward the trap region, namely those with $v_z < 0$. Under this assumption,
\begin{equation}
    N_{\mathrm{ODT}} =
    \int d^3r\, n_{\mathrm{MOT}}(\vec{r})\,
    \frac{1}{2}
    \int_{v_x^2+v_y^2<\frac{2|U|}{m}}
    dv_x\,dv_y\,
    \frac{1}{2\pi\mu^2}
    \exp\left(-\frac{v_x^2+v_y^2}{2\mu^2}\right).
\end{equation}

The velocity integral is more conveniently evaluated in polar coordinates in the transverse velocity plane. Using
\begin{equation}
    dv_x\,dv_y = \pi\,d(v_r^2),
\end{equation}
and introducing the variable
\begin{equation}
    x = \frac{v_r^2}{2\mu^2},
\end{equation}
one obtains
\begin{equation}
    \int_{0}^{\frac{2|U|}{m}}
    \pi\,d(v_r^2)\,
    \frac{1}{2\pi\mu^2}
    \exp\left(-\frac{v_r^2}{2\mu^2}\right)
    =
    \int_{0}^{\frac{|U|}{m\mu^2}} e^{-x}\,dx
    =
    1 - \exp\left(-\frac{|U_{\mathrm{ODT}}(\vec{r})|}{k_B T}\right).
\end{equation}

The expression for the number of trapped atoms therefore becomes
\begin{equation}
    N_{\mathrm{ODT}} =
    \int d^3r\, n_{\mathrm{MOT}}(\vec{r})\,
    \frac{1}{2}
    \left[
        1 - \exp\left(-\frac{|U_{\mathrm{ODT}}(\vec{r})|}{k_B T}\right)
    \right].
\end{equation}

In the shallow-trap limit, $|U_{\mathrm{ODT}}| \ll k_B T$, the exponential can be expanded to first order one then finds:
\begin{equation}
    N_{\mathrm{ODT}}
    \simeq
    \int d^3r\, n_{\mathrm{MOT}}(\vec{r})\,
    \frac{|U_{\mathrm{ODT}}(\vec{r})|}{2k_B T}.
\end{equation}

For a Gaussian dipole trap,
\begin{equation}
    U_{\mathrm{ODT}}(\vec{r}) =
    -U_0\,
    \frac{W_0^2}{W^2(z)}
    \exp\left(-\frac{2(x^2+y^2)}{W^2(z)}\right),
\end{equation}
so that
\begin{equation}
    N_{\mathrm{ODT}}
    \simeq
    \frac{U_0}{2k_B T}
    \int d^3r\, n_{\mathrm{MOT}}(\vec{r})\,
    \frac{W_0^2}{W^2(z)}
    \exp\left(-\frac{2(x^2+y^2)}{W^2(z)}\right).
\end{equation}

If the ODT waist is much smaller than the transverse size of the MOT, namely if $W(z) \ll R_x, R_y$, the transverse integrals can be extended to infinity:
\begin{equation}
    \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}
    \exp\left(-\frac{2(x^2+y^2)}{W^2(z)}\right)\,dx\,dy
    =
    \frac{\pi W^2(z)}{2}.
\end{equation}
Substituting this result into the previous expression leads to the cancellation of the $W^2(z)$ term:
\begin{equation}
    N_{\mathrm{ODT}}
    =
    \frac{U_0}{2k_B T}
    \frac{\pi W_0^2}{2}
    \int_{-R_z}^{R_z} n_{\mathrm{MOT}}(0,0,z)\,dz.
\end{equation}

To obtain a compact analytical estimate, the MOT is modeled as a uniform ellipsoid with radii $R_x$, $R_y$, and $R_z$, and constant density
\begin{equation}
    n_{\mathrm{MOT}} =
    \frac{3N_{\mathrm{MOT}}}{4\pi R_x R_y R_z}.
\end{equation}
Under this approximation,
\begin{equation}
    N_{\mathrm{ODT}}
    =
    \frac{U_0 \pi W_0^2}{4k_B T}
    \left(
        \frac{3N_{\mathrm{MOT}}}{4\pi R_x R_y R_z}\cdot 2R_z
    \right)
    =
    \frac{3}{8}
    \frac{U_0 W_0^2}{k_B T R_x R_y}
    N_{\mathrm{MOT}}.
\end{equation}
