\section{Injection into the Black Fiber}


The black-fiber injection branch was developed as an extension of the
existing $1064\,\mathrm{nm}$ Optical Dipole Trap setup described in
Section~\ref{sec:optical_setup}. In the original configuration, the light
generated by the IPG Photonics YLR-20-1064-LP-SF laser is prepared for
injection into the Hollow-Core Photonic Crystal Fiber (HCPCF), which guides
the ODT beam through the vacuum chamber.

Before reaching the HCPCF injection stage, the laser beam passes through an
optical isolator, which protects the laser source from back-reflections.
Half-wave plates and polarizing beam splitters are then used to regulate the
optical power and divide the beam between the available optical paths. In particular, HWP3 and PBS2 separate the light into two branches. The first
branch constitutes the existing HCPCF injection path: the beam passes through
an acousto-optic modulator (AOM) operating at $110\,\mathrm{MHz}$. The first
diffraction order is used for fast intensity control and frequency shifting,
whereas the zeroth order is blocked by a beam dump. The diffracted beam is
then expanded and collimated by a beam expander and finally steered by two
mirrors toward an $f=50\,\mathrm{mm}$ lens, which focuses it into the HCPCF.


The second output of PBS2 was reserved for injection into a conventional
telecom fiber, referred to throughout this work as the ``black fiber''. This
fiber provides the second optical field required for the realization of the
optical conveyor belt discussed in [REFERENCE TO THEORY SECTION]. The
conveyor belt is obtained by combining the beam guided through the HCPCF with
the counterpropagating beam delivered by the black fiber. Efficient and
stable injection into the latter is therefore a preliminary requirement for
the implementation of the complete conveyor-belt configuration.

The purpose of the measurements presented in this section was to establish
an efficient and reproducible method for coupling approximately
$1\,\mathrm{W}$ of $1064\,\mathrm{nm}$ light into the black fiber. The
injection strategy was initially developed on a dedicated test bench, where
the input-beam parameters and the focusing geometry could be characterized
independently of the complete ODT apparatus. Once the procedure had been
validated, the same strategy was transferred to the definitive branch
downstream of PBS2.

In addition to maximizing the transmitted power, the injection system had to
provide a well-defined spatial mode and a stable input polarization. These
requirements are essential because fluctuations in the power or
polarization of the beam emerging from the black fiber would directly affect
the depth and stability of the optical conveyor-belt potential. The
polarization stability is characterized separately in
Section~\ref{sec:black_fiber_polarization}.

Possible propagation losses associated with fiber bending were evaluated at
an early stage. The fiber was moderately bent while the transmitted power
was monitored, resulting in a power variation below $1\%$. Bending losses
were therefore considered negligible for the configurations used in the
following measurements.

\subsection{Gaussian-beam characterization procedure}

Throughout this work, the size and divergence of the optical beam were
characterized using a common beam-profiling procedure. The same method was
applied before and after the fiber whenever the injection geometry, input
beam parameters, or output wavefront had to be determined.

The beam under test was first aligned horizontally at a fixed height above
the optical table. This allowed its transverse intensity distribution to be
sampled at a sequence of longitudinal positions without introducing
additional optics between measurements. A camera mountedwas then moved along the propagation direction, and an
image of the beam cross-section was recorded at each position.

Each recorded image was cropped to isolate the beam spot from background
light and stray reflections. The resulting intensity distribution was fitted
with a two-dimensional Gaussian function, from which the beam radii along the
two transverse axes were extracted. Repeating this analysis at several
positions provided the beam radius as a function of the longitudinal
coordinate $z$.

For each transverse direction, the measured beam radius was fitted to the
standard Gaussian-beam propagation law

\begin{equation}
    w(z) =
    w_0
    \sqrt{
        1+
        \left(
            \frac{z-z_0}{z_R}
        \right)^2
    },
\end{equation}

where $w_0$ is the waist radius, $z_0$ is the waist position, and $z_R$ is
the associated Rayleigh range. This fit provided the size and position of the
beam waist to bedetermined. The reconstructed beam parameters could then be compared with
the target fiber-mode parameters or used to select and position the
injection optics.

\subsection{Standard injection strategy and preliminary setup}

In the standard approach to coupling a Gaussian beam into a single-mode
fiber, a collimated beam is sent onto a fiber collimator or focusing lens.
The optic focuses the incident beam to a waist located at the
fiber facet. Efficient coupling requires the focused field to match the
fundamental mode of the fiber in waist size, waist position, transverse
position.

When the nominally collimated beam was injected directly through the fixed
$f=18\,\mathrm{mm}$ fiber collimator, the measured injection efficiency was
only approximately $20\%$. The divergence of the beam incident on the
collimator was then varied by adjusting the upstream beam expander.

A significant improvement was observed when the beam expander was slightly
detuned from its collimated configuration, such that a weakly diverging beam
reached the fiber collimator. The required correction was approximately
equivalent to inserting a weak $f=-1000\,\mathrm{mm}$ lens before the
collimator. Under these conditions, the measured efficiency increased to
more than $50\%$.

This result was initially counterintuitive, since a fiber collimator is
nominally designed to focus a collimated input beam onto the fiber mode. It
therefore indicated that the optical position of the fiber mode did not
coincide with the focal plane expected from the nominal collimator geometry.


To investigate this behavior, the beam was characterized both before and
after the fiber using the profiling procedure described above. In
particular, the output beam was sampled at several longitudinal positions,
and its reconstructed waist was compared with the mode-field diameter
specified by the fiber manufacturer.

The origin of the longitudinal mismatch was attributed to the endcap present
at the fiber input. The endcap consists of a glass cone approximately
$300\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ long, spliced onto the fiber tip to improve power
handling. Its presence changes the optical distance between the external
focusing element and the effective fiber facet. Consequently, a collimated
beam incident on a fixed collimator does not necessarily produce a waist at
the correct axial position.

The observed improvement obtained with a diverging input is consistent with
this interpretation: the additional wavefront curvature shifts the focused
waist relative to that generated by a perfectly collimated input. However,
reproducing this divergence with a dedicated optical element would require a
long, weak lens with approximately $f=-1000\,\mathrm{mm}$ immediately before
the collimator. Such an element would be inconvenient to position, would
increase the complexity of the injection branch, and could introduce
additional aberrations or beam-quality degradation.

For this reason, the fixed $f=18\,\mathrm{mm}$ input collimator was replaced
by a more flexible focusing system based on an $f=25\,\mathrm{mm}$ lens
mounted on a micrometric translation stage. Translating this lens along the
propagation direction made it possible to adjust the longitudinal position
of the focused waist directly, without intentionally introducing divergence
into the input beam.

\subsection{Adjustable-lens injection setup}

The modified injection setup used a nominally collimated input beam whose
size was adjusted by the upstream beam expander. Two steering mirrors
controlled the beam position and propagation angle at the fiber input. The
beam was then focused by an $f=25\,\mathrm{mm}$ lens
(Thorlabs AC127-025-C), mounted on a micrometric translation stage along the
propagation axis. The axial degree of freedom was used to compensate for the
effective displacement introduced by the endcap.

At the opposite fiber end, a second collimator collected the transmitted
light. Depending on the measurement, the output beam was sent either to a
power meter to determine the transmitted power or to the movable camera used
for beam-profile characterization.

\subsection{Determination of the required input beam}

For a collimated Gaussian beam focused by a lens of focal length $f$, the
required incident beam radius $w_{\mathrm{in}}$ is related to the focused
waist radius $w_{\mathrm{f}}$ by

\begin{equation}
    w_{\mathrm{in}}
    =
    \frac{\lambda f}{\pi w_{\mathrm{f}}}.
\end{equation}

Using $\lambda=1.064\,\mu\mathrm{m}$,
$f=25\,\mathrm{mm}$, and a target fiber-mode radius of
$w_{\mathrm{f}}=5.2\,\mu\mathrm{m}$ gives

\begin{equation}
    w_{\mathrm{in}}
    =
    \frac{
        (1.064\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m})
        (25\times10^{-3}\,\mathrm{m})
    }{
        \pi
        (5.2\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m})
    }
    \approx
    1.6\,\mathrm{mm}.
\end{equation}

The beam expander was therefore adjusted to produce a nominally collimated
beam with a radius of approximately $1.6\,\mathrm{mm}$ at the injection
lens. The beam was profiled before injection to verify both its size and its
residual divergence.


The collimated beam prepared with the calculated radius was injected through
the $f=25\,\mathrm{mm}$ lens. 
The corrected lens position was validated through an additional
beam-profiling measurement. Light at $1064\,\mathrm{nm}$ and an optical power
of $7\,\mathrm{W}$ was propagated through the black fiber using the original
$f=18\,\mathrm{mm}$ collimator for injection and the adjustable
$f=25\,\mathrm{mm}$ lens at the output. This propagation direction was
therefore reversed relative to the earlier forward-injection measurement.

In this configuration, the $f=25\,\mathrm{mm}$ lens produced a correctly
collimated output beam. This confirmed that the lens had been placed at the
appropriate axial position relative to the effective fiber mode. 

With the corrected setup, the power measured after the black fiber and the
$f=18\,\mathrm{mm}$ output collimator reached approximately $70\%$ of the
power measured before the fiber, directly after the
$f=25\,\mathrm{mm}$ lens. Repeating the measurement yielded again an
efficiency of approximately $70\%$, demonstrating that the result was
reproducible.

During the same characterization, the fiber was flexed while the transmitted
power was monitored. The resulting power variations remained below $1\%$,
confirming that bending losses were negligible compared with the remaining
injection and optical losses.

\section{Polarization Stability Characterization}
\label{sec:black_fiber_polarization}

After optimizing the injection efficiency, the polarization stability of the
light transmitted through the black fiber was characterized. This quantity
is relevant to the optical conveyor belt because polarization fluctuations
can be converted into optical-power fluctuations by the
polarization-sensitive elements of the final setup, thereby affecting the
stability of the optical potential.

The two orthogonal linear-polarization components at the fiber output were
measured simultaneously. Their temporal stability was analyzed using the
Allan deviation, while their frequency content was investigated through the
power spectral density calculated from the Fourier transform of the recorded
signals. The measured noise spectrum was then compared with the expected
shot-noise level.

\subsection{Detection setup and polarization alignment}

The polarization-analysis setup is shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:polarization_setup}. The beam emerging from the black fiber
was collimated and sent through a half-wave plate (HWP) followed by a
polarizing beam splitter (PBS). The transmitted and reflected components
were detected simultaneously by two photodiodes.

During the initial alignment, the transmitted component was measured with a
home-made photodiode connected to channel~1, while the reflected component
was measured with a Thorlabs PDA100A2 connected to channel~2. The Thorlabs
detector was operated at $0\,\mathrm{dB}$ internal gain with
$6\,\mathrm{dB}$ of additional electrical attenuation. The total optical
power incident on the PBS was $4.2\,\mathrm{mW}$.

The HWP was first adjusted to obtain approximately equal power in the two PBS
outputs. This operating point, corresponding to a polarization orientation
close to $45^\circ$ relative to the PBS axes, provides high sensitivity to
small polarization rotations: a rotation transfers power from one output to
the other, producing anticorrelated variations in the two detector signals.

Before the stability measurements, the PBS orientation was optimized to
ensure that its finite extinction ratio did not limit the measurement. By
rotating the incident linear polarization and finely adjusting the PBS,
polarization extinction ratios of $31\,\mathrm{dB}$ in reflection and
$33\,\mathrm{dB}$ in transmission were obtained.

\subsection{Alignment to the fiber principal axes}

The polarization analyzer was then referenced to the principal axes of the
polarization-maintaining fiber. The setup consisted of an input HWP before
the fiber and, after the fiber, an output collimator, a steering mirror, a
second HWP, the PBS, and a power meter.

The two HWPs were adjusted to maximize transmission through the PBS while
minimizing reflection. This condition corresponds to the excitation of one
of the principal axes of the fiber followed by the alignment of the emerging
linear polarization with one of the PBS axes. The optimum input-HWP angle was
$131^\circ$.

The output HWP was adjusted to obtain a maximum transmitted power of
$2.68\,\mathrm{mW}$. The power meter was then moved to the reflection port,
where the signal was minimized by jointly fine-tuning the PBS orientation
and the two HWPs. A minimum reflected power of $4\,\mu\mathrm{W}$ was
measured, including a background of $1.5\,\mu\mathrm{W}$, while the maximum
reflected power was approximately $2\,\mathrm{mW}$. Without background
subtraction, the corresponding polarization extinction ratio was

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{PER}
    =
    10\log_{10}
    \left(
        \frac{P_{\mathrm{max}}}{P_{\mathrm{min}}}
    \right)
    =
    10\log_{10}
    \left(
        \frac{2\,\mathrm{mW}}{4\,\mu\mathrm{W}}
    \right)
    \approx 27\,\mathrm{dB}.
\end{equation}

As an additional check, the fiber transmission efficiency was measured again.
The input and output powers were $4.18\,\mathrm{mW}$ and
$2.88\,\mathrm{mW}$, respectively, corresponding to

\begin{equation}
    \eta_{\mathrm{fiber}}
    =
    \frac{2.88\,\mathrm{mW}}{4.18\,\mathrm{mW}}
    \approx 69\%.
\end{equation}

This value is consistent with the efficiency measured during the injection
optimization.

\subsection{Data acquisition and normalized polarization signal}

For the final stability measurements, the home-made detector was placed on
the reflected port and connected to channel~1. The Thorlabs PDA100A2 was
placed on the transmitted port and connected to channel~2, operated at
$10\,\mathrm{dB}$ internal gain without external attenuation.

For each measurement, $10^5$ samples were acquired simultaneously from both
channels. Traces with total durations of $600\,\mathrm{ms}$,
$60\,\mathrm{s}$, and $600\,\mathrm{s}$ were recorded to investigate
different timescales. The $60\,\mathrm{s}$ acquisition was performed both in
standard sampling mode, to preserve faster fluctuations, and in
High-Resolution mode, to improve the resolution of slower measurements.

After background subtraction and detector calibration, the measured
voltages were converted into the transmitted and reflected powers
$P_{\mathrm{T}}(t)$ and $P_{\mathrm{R}}(t)$. The total detected power was

\begin{equation}
    P_{\Sigma}(t)
    =
    P_{\mathrm{T}}(t)+P_{\mathrm{R}}(t),
\end{equation}

while the normalized differential signal was defined as

\begin{equation}
    D(t)
    =
    \frac{
        P_{\mathrm{T}}(t)-P_{\mathrm{R}}(t)
    }{
        P_{\mathrm{T}}(t)+P_{\mathrm{R}}(t)
    }.
    \label{eq:normalized_polarization_difference}
\end{equation}

A fluctuation of the total transmitted power produces mainly a variation in
$P_{\Sigma}(t)$, whereas a polarization fluctuation transfers power between
the two PBS outputs and therefore appears predominantly in $D(t)$. The
normalization suppresses common-mode power fluctuations to first order.

\subsection{Allan-deviation analysis}

The time-domain stability of the polarization signal was quantified using the
overlapping Allan deviation. For a uniformly sampled sequence $D_k$ with
sampling interval $\tau_0$, averages were calculated over adjacent groups of
$m$ samples,

\begin{equation}
    \overline{D}_j(\tau)
    =
    \frac{1}{m}
    \sum_{k=jm}^{(j+1)m-1}D_k,
    \qquad
    \tau=m\tau_0.
\end{equation}

The Allan variance was then calculated from the difference between
consecutive averages,

\begin{equation}
    \sigma_D^2(\tau)
    =
    \frac{1}{2}
    \left\langle
        \left[
            \overline{D}_{j+1}(\tau)
            -
            \overline{D}_{j}(\tau)
        \right]^2
    \right\rangle,
\end{equation}

and the Allan deviation was obtained as

\begin{equation}
    \sigma_D(\tau)
    =
    \sqrt{\sigma_D^2(\tau)}.
\end{equation}

The Allan deviation expresses the typical change in the average polarization
signal between consecutive intervals of duration $\tau$. It therefore
reveals the timescales over which averaging improves the polarization
stability and those over which slow drift begins to dominate.

A decreasing Allan deviation indicates that averaging suppresses the
dominant fluctuations. A minimum identifies the optimum averaging time,
whereas an increase at longer $\tau$ indicates the onset of slow drift or
other low-frequency instabilities. The logarithmic slope of
$\sigma_D(\tau)$ can also be used to distinguish different classes of noise.

The Allan deviation was evaluated for the individual polarization channels,
the total-power signal $P_{\Sigma}(t)$, and the normalized differential
signal $D(t)$. Comparing these quantities makes it possible to determine
whether the observed instability is dominated by common-mode optical-power
noise or by a genuine redistribution of power between the two polarization
components.

\subsection{Frequency-domain analysis and shot-noise comparison}

The frequency content of the measured power fluctuations was analyzed by
computing the power spectral density (PSD) of the calibrated photodiode
signal from its discrete Fourier transform. Before calculating the Fourier
transform, the mean value was subtracted from each time trace. The resulting
PSD describes how the variance of the measured optical power is distributed
over frequency.

The measured power-noise spectrum was compared with the quantum shot-noise
limit expected for a coherent optical field at the same average power. For a
photodiode with responsivity $\mathcal{R}$ receiving an average optical power
$\overline{P}$, the mean photocurrent is

\begin{equation}
    \overline{I}
    =
    \mathcal{R}\,\overline{P}.
\end{equation}

The single-sided shot-noise PSD of the photocurrent is

\begin{equation}
    S_{I,\mathrm{SN}}
    =
    2e\overline{I}
    =
    2e\mathcal{R}\,\overline{P},
\end{equation}

where $e$ is the elementary charge. The corresponding equivalent
optical-power PSD is obtained by dividing by the squared photodiode
responsivity:

\begin{equation}
    S_{P,\mathrm{SN}}
    =
    \frac{S_{I,\mathrm{SN}}}{\mathcal{R}^2}
    =
    \frac{2e\overline{P}}{\mathcal{R}}.
    \label{eq:shot_noise_power_psd}
\end{equation}

Using the relation

\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{R}
    =
    \frac{\mathrm{QE}\,e}{\hbar\omega},
\end{equation}

the same limit can be expressed as

\begin{equation}
    S_{P,\mathrm{SN}}
    =
    \frac{2\hbar\omega\,\overline{P}}{\mathrm{QE}},
\end{equation}

where $\mathrm{QE}$ is the photodiode quantum efficiency. In the ideal case
of unit quantum efficiency, this reduces to the quantum limit

\begin{equation}
    S_{P,\mathrm{QL}}
    =
    2\hbar\omega\,\overline{P}.
    \label{eq:quantum_shot_noise_limit}
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:quantum_shot_noise_limit} was used as the reference
shot-noise level for comparison with the measured optical-power PSD. Both
quantities were expressed using the same single-sided spectral convention
and in units of $\mathrm{W^2/Hz}$. A measured spectrum above this limit
indicates the presence of excess technical noise in addition to the
fundamental photon shot noise.

If the spectra are instead plotted as amplitude spectral densities, the
corresponding quantum limit is

\begin{equation}
    \sqrt{S_{P,\mathrm{QL}}}
    =
    \sqrt{2\hbar\omega\,\overline{P}},
\end{equation}

with units of $\mathrm{W/\sqrt{Hz}}$.
